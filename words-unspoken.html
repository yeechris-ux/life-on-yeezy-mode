Words Unspoken

Don't let the things you want to say to the ones that matter go unheard.

2023 taught me a lot about my ability — and inability — to sit with stress, name my emotions, and be vulnerable. My plan for the year was to leave the company I'd started in 2016 (and sold in 2021), spend a couple of months in Europe, then take my dad on a month-long US road trip in a camper van, with our two dogs, to see the leaves change on the east coast — one of his lifelong wants. Life had other plans.

In June, my father was diagnosed with Lewy Body Dementia. It seemed, at first, like we'd caught it early. It didn't stay that way. Over the following month he became irritable and paranoid, developed motor changes, memory loss, and visual hallucinations. He started medication, and I made the call to finish out my trip through August — I was burned out after six years of running the business and needed the break. The medication helped in the short term, but his condition kept shifting, and by the final weeks of my trip he needed 24-hour supervision. That weight fell on my mom and sisters, and I felt awful not being there — even knowing there was nothing I could have done differently, and while dealing with my own mental health on top of it. Selfish, sad, angry, frustrated, helpless — all of it, at once.

Once I got back, I tried to pull together whatever support I could to take pressure off my mom and sisters, stayed with my dad, and eventually we found him a care home that could handle his medical needs. Watching the disease move that fast made it clear this was the beginning of the end, and it forced me to really sit with our relationship over the years.

My dad taught me a lot — hard work, care, humility — mostly by example, rarely by explanation. Growing up we clashed constantly; it felt like I could never do what he wanted or make him proud. It took me years to understand what he'd actually been trying to teach me. I think we'd have been closer if he'd told me the why behind it instead of leaving me to guess.

Lesson: tell the people who matter to you how you feel, and why. We assume our actions make it obvious. They don't, unless we actually say it.

Reading The Gift of You by Dr. Bill McCord, I recognized a pattern in my own relationships — assuming everyone sees things through the same lens I do, instead of explaining my actual reasoning. A lot of avoidable conflict has come from exactly that gap.

I only saw my dad cry twice — both at my grandparents' funerals. We never once talked about feelings. I absorbed the idea that expressing anything made me look weak, and I've cried maybe a handful of times since leaving home at seventeen. Instead, all of that showed up sideways — depression, anxiety, explosive anger, even physical symptoms like hair loss and flare-ups of my Ankylosing Spondylitis. I regret how much my past partners had to absorb because I hadn't done that work yet.

Over the last two years — through reflection, reading, therapy, and finally talking more openly — I've built better tools for actually sitting with those feelings. It's a hard way to get there, but caring for my dad gave us the chance to talk about things we never had: passion, marriage, fatherhood, love, what any of it means. I don't know how much of it he'll remember. I wish I'd shared more of my own joys and fears with him, and told him more plainly what he means to me.

Lesson: it's okay to be vulnerable. Bearing your emotions doesn't make you less capable of showing up for the people who need you — it's what lets you actually do it. It's never too late to start.

My dad never spent time or money on himself — always delaying gratification, always finding a project instead of a trip or a fishing day. As his mobility narrowed, I'd take him for walks or push his wheelchair, and we talked about regrets. He told me he had none — that he was grateful for the travel, the fishing, the family time he did have. He'd always been more present than his habits suggested; his contentment wasn't about some future payoff, it was already real.

He's now in the later stages of the disease, and comfort care is what's left. It's hard knowing he won't meet a future wife or grandchildren, that we'll never finish that road trip or go fishing again — but I hold onto what we did share. He's seventy-one. I thought I had another decade with him. I didn't. I should have made more time, reached out more, been more present in the moments I had.

Lesson: life is unpredictable and fleeting. Appreciate the small moments, stay content, but don't keep delaying the things — and the people — that matter.
