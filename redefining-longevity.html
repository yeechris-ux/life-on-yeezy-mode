Redefining Longevity

The simple ways to live a more enriched and longer life.

Every one of us has a finite amount of time. This is about the lifestyle shifts worth considering to extend not just how long we live (lifespan), but how long we live well — free of chronic disease (healthspan).

The CDC puts average life expectancy at roughly 73 for men and 79 for women. My father recently passed at 71, which made that statistic land differently — it doesn't account for quality of life in someone's later years. Since COVID upended his routine in 2020 — he'd retired, weighing the risk of the virus against the value of social interaction, mental stimulation, and physical activity — I noticed lifestyle shifts that likely contributed to his decline, and it changed how I think about longevity entirely.

After his Lewy Body Dementia diagnosis, I went looking for anything that might explain the trajectory, which led me to Dan Buettner's The Blue Zones and the Netflix documentary Live to 100.

Lesson: we control most of what shapes our physical and mental capacity. Habits and lifestyle can dramatically change both the length and quality of our lives — and longevity doesn't appear to track with wealth.

Buettner studies five regions with the highest concentrations of centenarians: Sardinia's Barbagia region, Ikaria in Greece, the Nicoya Peninsula in Costa Rica, Loma Linda in California, and Okinawa in Japan. None of them are wealthy by conventional measures — if anything, they have less material wealth and more overall satisfaction. A few of the through-lines:

Move naturally. Instead of gym memberships, most Blue Zone residents build low-intensity movement into daily life — gardening, housework, walking. With 150–220 minutes of low-to-moderate activity a week, you can counter what's now called "sitting disease," an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease, cancer, and type 2 diabetes.

Have a purpose. Adam Grant calls the drift into joylessness "languishing." I've hit stretches where I felt like a boat without a rudder — new hobbies, challenges, and curiosity-driven work are often the way back to what he calls "flow."

Down-shift. Chronic stress drives inflammation, high blood pressure, and heart disease. Blue Zone communities build in routines — prayer, meditation, naps, happy hour — to actively manage it.

The 80% rule. Okinawans stop eating at roughly 80% full. With food delivery making overconsumption effortless, it's a useful check — 42% of US adults and 27% of Canadian adults are now classified as obese.

Plant slant. Heavy on vegetables, beans, whole grains, fruit, nuts, fish, and poultry; light on red meat and sugar — the Mediterranean diet overlaps here.

Wine, in moderation — some benefit may come from antioxidants, some simply from the social ritual of sharing a drink with people you love. It's not for everyone, and the tradeoffs are worth understanding.

Belonging. The US Surgeon General has flagged that roughly half of American adults report measurable loneliness, linked to a 29% higher risk of heart disease, 32% higher stroke risk, and 50% higher dementia risk in older adults. Most Blue Zone centenarians are part of some faith community.

Loved ones first. Many Blue Zone families live multigenerationally, and the health outcomes for everyone in the household — including kids — improve. Countries like Singapore now offer financial incentives for families to live near aging parents.

Right tribe. Jim Rohn's line about being the average of the five people you spend the most time with holds up here — the habits of your closest circle are contagious, for better or worse.

Lesson: the Blue Zone lifestyle is a framework, not a mandate. Small steps toward it, rather than a total overhaul, can meaningfully shift where you land.

I don't think there's a single explanation for how quickly my father declined. But I do think some of these shifts might have bought him more time. I don't need to live forever — I want to live as fully as possible. I hope this gives you a few ideas for doing the same.
